# librarian — what did you mark and never use?

The Librarian looks for relationships between what you marked and what you wrote. It is built to
refuse most of what it finds, because the first version of this idea was tested and failed.

## The result that shaped it

The original thesis was a graph of connections *between books*. Twenty-eight candidate edges were
generated by four independent lenses and attacked by three adversarial verifiers each. Twenty-seven
refutations stand. One was overturned because two of the three skeptics had read a stale derived
file instead of the raw text, and that edge is a candidate again, not a finding. Zero verified
cross-book edges. See `docs/reference/DECISIONS.md`.

So the cross-book graph is not on the roadmap. What survived is narrower and checkable: **did an
idea you marked ever leave a receipt in your own writing?**

## candidates.py — the corroboration matcher

```bash
READER_SURFACES=/path/to/your/writing python agents/librarian/candidates.py
```

Reads the merged corpus, takes up to three distinctive phrases from every mark, and streams your
own files looking for them. Three answers per mark:

| status | meaning |
|---|---|
| `unapplied` | the phrase appears nowhere in your writing — an idea marked and never spent |
| `noise` | the phrase appears in more files than the floor allows; its presence proves nothing |
| `candidate` | the phrase appears somewhere — a **question for a judge**, never a finding |

**Read that last row twice.** On the reference library of 88 books and 5,211 marks against 2,235
of the reader's own files, this stage produced 21 candidates. Read by hand, about five were a
real idea travelling from a book into the work — a named practice from a management book turning
up in an operating document, a framework's own term of art reused in a plan. The rest were phrases
of the English language that happen to occur in both places: "a leap of faith", "does the heavy
lifting", "vp of engineering". Every match was true. Most meant nothing.

Tightening did not fix it. Requiring each phrase to carry a token rare *within the library*
removed four of twenty-five and left the coincidences untouched, because those phrases are
genuinely rare inside a shelf of business books while being common in English. That is the
boundary of a phrase matcher, and naming it is more useful than hiding it: **this stage is recall.**
It finds every place a mark could have been used, cheaply, with no model and no network, and a
judge decides which are real.

`unapplied` is the robust half, and it needs no judge: 5,189 of 5,211 marks had no verbatim receipt
anywhere. The report ranks your books by unspent marks, which is the question the whole pipeline
exists to answer.

## The five rules, and the failure behind each

| rule | why |
|---|---|
| never one or two words | "the system", "first principles" match everything; a two-word match is a coincidence with good manners |
| at least two non-stop tokens | otherwise "of the same as" is a candidate |
| the stoplist is corpus-derived too | a shelf of management books says "leader" everywhere; a hand-written stoplist cannot know that |
| a noise floor: a phrase in more than max(25, 2%) of your files | the ancestor of this check matched a phrase in 782 files and called all 782 evidence |
| a mirror floor: a file matching more than 10 distinct phrases | one review file holding the scanned pages supplied 60 of the first 102 receipts — every one the book quoting itself. Path rules cannot catch it; the file had an ordinary name in an ordinary directory |

A phrase in too many files is noise. A file with too many phrases is a mirror. The two floors are
the same idea seen from each side.

## Not built

`judge.md` and `refute.md` (an agent that adjudicates a candidate, refuting by default, quoting
both sides), and `write.py` (which would touch only a reserved `related:` field). Designed in
`docs/reference/AGENT-DESIGNS.md`; not in this release.
